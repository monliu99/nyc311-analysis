Weather does line up with 311 complaints, although the stronger link is with what people complain about rather than with how many complaints come in overall. Having merged NOAA's daily Central Park weather onto 4,318 complete days and compared like with like (days in the same month of the same year, on the same weekday, with holidays accounted for), we find that a night below 20°F goes with about 35% more complaints in total but almost ten times as many heat and hot water complaints (+893%) as a night with a low in the 50s (the second chart below). This means that the Office of Emergency Management can likely plan around a few weather-sensitive complaint groups instead of the citywide total.

Rain shows up most clearly in sewer and tree complaints, some of it a day late, as a day with half an inch of rain or more goes with 133% more sewer and flooding complaints (and 39% more the next day) and 27% more damaged and dead tree complaints (36% more the day after, likely as people find the damage once the storm has passed). Snow works with a delay as well, since snow and ice complaints are 509% higher the day after a snowfall of an inch or more, while pothole complaints dip 37% on the snow day itself and peak instead in March.

Temperature changes the content the most, as heat and hot water makes up 34% of complaints on nights below 20°F and about 1% on nights of 70°F or more, while noise goes the other way (10% against 20%, raw shares in which the season also plays a part). For staffing, the seasonal profile in the last chart is likely the most usable piece, with heat complaints averaging 1,413 a day in January against 71 in August, and noise peaking in June at 1,542 a day.

These are associations from a regression that holds the month, the weekday and holidays fixed, not proof that the weather causes the complaints, since weather also changes who is outside to notice a problem and who picks up the phone (noise complaints drop 20% on heavy-rain days, for example). In addition, one Central Park station stands in for all five boroughs, so a storm that hits Staten Island harder than Manhattan gets blurred, and a borough-level version of this analysis would mainly need weather from more stations.
