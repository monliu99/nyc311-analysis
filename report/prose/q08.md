The dashboard at the end of this section is a single web page that staff can open in any browser, with a map of the 59 community districts that they can zoom into, hover over for a district's complaint rate and rank, and click for a short profile. It covers about 24.5 million complaints from January 2010 to November 19, 2021, shaded by complaints per 1,000 residents a year (the same measure as Q7), and at street level it shows individual complaints from the most recent 30 complete days.

For a council office preparing for a community board meeting, the click is likely the most useful part, since the profile lists the district's top five complaint types, how quickly its complaints close, the share still unresolved, and each year's rate (which shows whether things are getting better or worse), while the buttons at the bottom right switch the shading to one of the four largest complaint categories (noise, housing conditions, traffic and parking, streets and sidewalks) to show where a particular problem clusters.

The profile reports time to close in two ways, and the second is worth reading first, as it compares the district with the citywide typical time for the same complaint types and years (the mix adjustment used in Q4). Midtown (Manhattan 5) is a good example, since its median complaint takes 47.6 hours to close against 40.6 hours citywide, which looks slow, yet it closes the same kinds of complaints 8% faster than typical, which means that the longer median comes from the mix of complaints it receives rather than from slower service.

When reading the map, it helps to keep in mind that rates are per resident, so Midtown and other districts with many workers and visitors look high (see Q7); the individual complaints are a random sample of 20,000 of the 228,617 located complaints in that 30-day window (about 9%), so the numbers on the map's circles count sampled complaints, not all of them; and the 1,182,353 complaints with no community district are left out of the shading. The map is also a snapshot of the data as delivered, so keeping it current would mean rerunning the pipeline on each new extract.
